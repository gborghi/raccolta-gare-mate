*Probability at the bar*

![[src_gs_2014__prob12.png]]

> Probability at the Bar (40 points)
> MISTERIUS has managed to recover the video of the famous dice game between Bagolot and Enrico Fermi at a bar in Göttingen in 1923. As can be seen in the video, Bagolot and Fermi have three dice: one with 4 faces, numbered from 1 to 4; another with 6 faces, numbered from 1 to 6; and the last die with 8 faces, numbered from 1 to 8. Bagolot challenges Fermi to the following gamble: Fermi rolls all the dice once; at that point, Bagolot discards one die; Fermi then rolls the two remaining dice a second time. All the points obtained are added together (from the first roll of the three dice and from the second roll with the two remaining dice): if the total is 15, Fermi wins 100 marks from Bagolot; otherwise, Bagolot wins 10 marks from Fermi. It can be seen that Fermi rolls all three dice together once, getting 8 as the sum of the faces. Unfortunately, the video ends abruptly; it is known that, at that point, something astonishing happened: Bagolot assessed his chances of winning the 10 marks perfectly depending on which die he would discard. What was his best chance of winning the 10 marks? [Write as the solution the first 4 digits after the decimal point of the result.]

*Bagolot's Pastime*

> Bagolot's Pastime (50 points)
> Bagolot's favorite pastime consisted of taking a sheet of paper and cutting it so as to obtain a rectangle in which one side was 4/7 as long as the other. *The Bagolot Hypothesis*

![[src_gs_2014__prob20.png]]

> The Bagolot Hypothesis (points 50)
> In his arithmetic studies, Bagolot considered numerical operations different from the canonical ones. For example, the famous Bagolot Hypothesis concerns the operation of the contour of a number: Bagolot defined the additive contour of n as the sum of the digits of n, and the multiplicative contour of n as the product of the digits of n. He then introduced the (general) contour c(n) of n as the sum of the additive contour of n and the multiplicative contour of n: for example, c(3) = 6 and c(13) = 7. The IB is as follows: for every positive integer n less than 100, the number 11 appears in the sequence n, c(n), c(c(n)), c(c(c(n))), . . . , c(· · (c(n)) · ·) [c applied k times], . . . . Bagolot's mathematical colleagues doubted the IB because—they declared—there are too many counterexamples; but as is well known: mathematicians are always envious. Besides, how many counterexamples to the IB are there, after all?

*The Millionaire's Third Problem*

> The Millionaire's Third Problem (70 points)
> The third problem on the way to becoming millionaires is as follows: Consider a square chessboard with sides 9 dm long (we told you that mathematicians play chess!). At one of the four vertices of the chessboard, there is an ant. The chessboard is divided into 81 square squares, each 1 dm on a side, giving a total of 100 vertices. There are 4 pegs on the chessboard, located at as many vertices (among the 100 possible ones). The ant moves in a straight line toward the nearest peg. Once it reaches that peg, it heads toward the nearest of the pegs that have not yet been visited, always in a straight line; if two pegs are at the same distance, it chooses one of the two at random. It continues in this way until it reaches the last peg. Given that the pegs are positioned so that the path the ant can take in this way is maximal, how many millimeters long is the path it has taken?
