*Prove a nonconstant polynomial with nonneg coefficients whose power is positive is itself positive*

> We say that a polynomial $f$ is *positive* if it can be written as the product of two nonconstant polynomials with all real coefficients greater than or equal to $0$. Let $f(x)$ be a nonconstant polynomial with nonnegative coefficients such that $f^n$ is positive for some positive integer $n$. Prove that $f(x)$ is positive.
> 
> **Remark.** The problem statement is incorrect. The hypothesis that the leading coefficient of $f$ is non-zero (i.e., $f$ is non-zero) was missing. Alternatively, the conclusion that $f(x)$ is positive is false as stated, since $f(x) = cx$ with $c$ a positive integer is a counterexample. Students who give an answer correcting the problem by adding a hypothesis such as $f(1) = cx$ with $c$ a positive integer, as well as those who correct the problem by adding such a hypothesis as mentioned above and then solve it, will receive full marks together with an apology from the jury.

*Permutation of points on half-disk boundary minimising sum of squared distances*

> Let $X = \{(x,y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : x \ge 0,\, x^2 + y^2 = 1\} \cup \{(x,0) : -1 \le x \le 1\}$ be the boundary of the closed half-disk of radius $1$.
> 
> a) Let $n > 1$ be an integer and $P_1, P_2, \ldots, P_n \in X$. Prove that there exists a permutation $\sigma : \{1, 2, \ldots, n\} \to \{1, 2, \ldots, n\}$ such that
> $$\sum_{i=1}^{n} |P_{\sigma(i+1)} - P_{\sigma(i)}|^2 \le 8,$$
> where we define $\sigma(n+1) = \sigma(1)$.
> 
> b) Determine all subsets $\{P_1, P_2, \ldots, P_n\} \subset X$ such that for any permutation $\sigma : \{1, 2, \ldots, n\} \to \{1, 2, \ldots, n\}$,
> $$\sum_{i=1}^{n} |P_{\sigma(i+1)} - P_{\sigma(i)}|^2 \ge 8$$
> (where $\sigma(n+1) = \sigma(1)$).

*Distance inequality involving rows of a real d×n matrix and subspace supremum*

> Let $d \le n$ be positive integers and $A$ a real $d \times n$ matrix. We view $A$ as a linear transformation $\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^d$ given by $v \mapsto Av$ (viewing elements of $\mathbb{R}^n$ as column vectors). Let $\sigma(A)$ be the supremum over all $d$-dimensional subspaces $W$ of $\mathbb{R}^n$ of $\inf_{w \in W,\, |w|=1} |Aw|$. For each $i \in \{1, \ldots, d\}$, let $r(i) \in \mathbb{R}^n$ be the $i$-th row-vector of $A$, and for $j \in \{1, \ldots, d\}$ with $j \ne i$, let $d(r(i), r(j))$ denote the Euclidean distance between $r(i)$ and $r(j)$.
> 
> For each $i \in \{1, \ldots, d\}$, let $e_i$ be the $i$-th element of the canonical basis of $\mathbb{R}^d$. Prove that
> $$\sigma(A) \le \min_{i} \min_{j : j \ne i} d(r(i), r(j)) \le \sqrt{n} \cdot \sigma(A).$$
> 
> **Remark.** $|\cdot|$ denotes the Euclidean norm; $A^T$ is the transpose of $A$; $d(r(i), r(j))$, $1 \le j \le d$, $j \ne i$, denotes the distance of $r(i)$ from $r(j)$.

*Words over {a,b}: m-equivalence of two words of length l via subword deletion*

> We consider here words over the alphabet $\{a, b\}$: sequences of $a$'s and $b$'s of finite length. We write $v \preceq w$ if $v$ is a *subword* of $w$, that is, $v$ can be obtained from $w$ by deleting some letters (for example, $aba \preceq abba$). We say that two words $x$ and $y$ are *$m$-equivalent* if $u \preceq x$ as well as $u \preceq y$, or neither $u \preceq x$ nor $u \preceq y$, for every word $u$ with $|u| \le m$ (i.e., whether $u$ is a subword of $x$ is the same as whether $u$ is a subword of $y$, for all words $u$ of length at most $m$).
> 
> Let $l$ and $m$ be positive integers.
> 
> a) Prove that if $2m \le l$, then there exist two distinct words $x$ and $y$ of length $l$ that are $m$-equivalent.
> 
> b) Prove that if $2m > l$, then two distinct words $x$ and $y$ of length $l$ cannot be $m$-equivalent.

*Parolle sopra {a,b}: m-equivalenza di due parole di lunghezza l tramite cancellazione delle sottoscritte*

> Prendiamo qui le parole sopra l'alfabeto $\{a, b\}$: sequenze di $a$ e $b$ di lunghezza finita. Scriviamo $v \preceq w$ se $v$ è una sottoscrizione ** di $w$, cioè $v$ può essere ottenuto da $w$ eliminando alcune lettere (ad esempio, $aba \preceq abba$). Diciamo che due parole $x$ e $y$ sono *$m$-equivalenti* se $u \preceq x$ così come $u \preceq y$, o né $u \preceq x$ né $u \preceq y$, per ogni parola $u$ con $|u| \le m$ (cioè, se $u$ è una sottoscrizione di $x$ è lo stesso che se $u$ è una sottoscrizione di $y$, per tutte le parole $u$ di lunghezza massima $m$).
> 
> Lasciate che $l$ e $m$ siano integri positivi.
> 
> a) dimostrare che se $2m \le l$, esistono due parole distinte $x$ e $y$ di lunghezza $l$ che sono equivalenti a $m$.
> 
> b) dimostrare che se $2m > l$, allora due parole distinte $x$ e $y$ di lunghezza $l$ non possono essere $m$-equivalenti.
