*Colorful integers on a circular arrangement; find maximum non-colorful*

> Let $n$ be an integer with $n \ge 3$. An integer $m \ge n+1$ is called **$n$-colorful** if there exists a way to write one integer from $\{1, 2, \ldots, n\}$ in each of $m$ cells arranged in a circle such that the following condition holds:
> 
> > For any $n+1$ consecutive cells and for any integer $i$ with $1 \le i \le n$, there exists a cell among those $n+1$ cells containing the value $i$.
> 
> Prove that there are only finitely many positive integers that are not $n$-colorful, and find the largest such integer.

*Interi colorati disposti in cerchio; trova il massimo non colorato*

> Sia $n$ un numero intero con $n \ge 3$. Un numero intero $m \ge n+1$ è detto **$n$-colorato** se esiste un modo per scrivere un numero intero tratto da $\{1, 2, \ldots, n\}$ in ciascuna delle $m$ celle disposte in cerchio, in modo che valga la seguente condizione:
>
> > Per qualsiasi $n+1$ celle consecutive e per qualsiasi intero $i$ con $1 \le i \le n$, esiste una cella tra quelle $n+1$ celle che contiene il valore $i$.
>
> Dimostrare che esistono solo un numero finito di interi positivi che non sono $n$-colorati, e determina il più grande tra questi interi.

*Minimum value of sum of floor(a_i/i) over all permutations*

> Let $n$ be a positive integer. When $a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_n$ is a permutation of $1, 2, \ldots, n$, find the minimum possible value of

*Bead-moving game on a line with ratio r; find r allowing red bead to reach B in at most 2021 moves*

> Let $r > 1$ be a rational number, and let $B$ and $R$ be two distinct points on a line. Initially, one blue bead is placed at $B$ and one red bead is placed at $R$. Taro repeatedly performs the following operation:

> > Choose an integer $k$ and a point $X$ at which one of the beads is placed. Let $Y$ be the point where the other bead is placed. On the ray $YX$, choose the point $X'$ such that $YX' = r^k \cdot YX$. Then move the bead at $X$ to $X'$.
> 
> Find all values of $r$ such that Taro can move the red bead to $B$ in at most $2021$ operations. Here $PQ$ denotes the length of segment $PQ$.

*Minimum operations for chairman to achieve majority preference ordering*

> Let $m$ be an odd integer with $m \ge 3$, and let $n$ be an integer with $n \ge 3$. For each integer $i$ with $1 \le i \le m$, let $a_{i,1}, a_{i,2}, \ldots, a_{i,n}$ be a permutation of $1, 2, \ldots, n$.
> 
> A chairman and $m$ delegates (delegate $1$, delegate $2$, $\ldots$, delegate $m$) are holding a meeting to select problems for this year's IMO. There are $n$ candidate problems (problem $1$, problem $2$, $\ldots$, problem $n$). Each delegate has a **preference score** for each problem, which is an integer from $1$ to $n$; the initial preference score of delegate $i$ for problem $j$ is $a_{i,j}$. The chairman can repeatedly perform the following operation:
> 
> > Choose integers $i, j, k$ with $1 \le i \le m$ and $1 \le j, k \le n$ such that the difference between delegate $i$'s preference scores for problem $j$ and problem $k$ is exactly $1$, and swap those two preference scores of delegate $i$.
> 
> Regardless of the values of the $mn$ integers $a_{i,j}$ ($1 \le i \le m$, $1 \le j \le n$), find the minimum non-negative integer $L$ such that the chairman can achieve the following condition by performing the operation at most $L$ times:
> 
> > For any two distinct integers $x, y$ with $1 \le x, y \le n$, there exist an integer $s \ge 2$ and a sequence $p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_s$ of integers from $1$ to $n$ with $p_1 = x$ and $p_s = y$, such that for every integer $t$ with $1 \le t \le s-1$, at least $\dfrac{m+1}{2}$ delegates have a strictly higher preference score for problem $p_{t+1}$ than for problem $p_t$.

*Hunter-rabbit game on infinite grid; can rabbit avoid being located?*

> A hunter and an invisible rabbit play a game on an infinite grid. First, the hunter chooses a positive integer $k$ and assigns one integer from $\{1, 2, \ldots, k\}$ to each cell of the grid, then tells the assignment to the rabbit. Next, the rabbit chooses a cell to hide in. After that, the rabbit repeatedly performs the following actions in order:
> 
> (i) The rabbit tells the hunter the integer assigned to the cell it is currently in.
> 
> (ii) The rabbit moves to an unvisited cell sharing a side with its current cell. If no such unvisited cell exists, the game ends.
> 
> At any point during the game, if the hunter can uniquely identify the cell where the rabbit first hid, using only the integers reported so far, then the game ends at that point.
> 
> Is it possible for the rabbit to choose its starting cell and continue playing without the game ending, regardless of how the hunter chooses $k$ and the assignment of integers?
