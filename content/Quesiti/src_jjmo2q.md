*Cryptarithm: IMO plus JMO plus ampersand terms equals JJMO*

> Solve the following cryptarithm and write the number corresponding to $\mathrm{IMO}$. The addition is carried out column-wise as shown (each row's digits are right-aligned):
> $$\begin{array}{cccc} & I & M & O \\ & & & \& \\ & J & M & O \\ & & & \& \\ + & & & \& \\ \hline J & J & M & O \end{array}$$
> Here, solving a cryptarithm means determining the correspondence between letters and digits, according to the conditions below, so that the above equality holds.
> - Each of $I, M, O, \&$ corresponds to one of the digits $0, 1, 2, \ldots, 9$. The same letter corresponds to the same digit, and different letters correspond to different digits. Thus $\mathrm{IMO}$ denotes the $3$-digit number formed by writing the digit corresponding to $I$, the digit corresponding to $M$, and the digit corresponding to $O$, in that order.
> - The leading letters $I, \&, J$ do not correspond to $0$.
> - The digit corresponding to $I$ is not necessarily $1$, and the digit corresponding to $O$ is not necessarily $0$.

*Crittografia: IMO più JMO più ampersand termini uguali a JJMO*

> Risolvi il seguente criptaritmo e scrivi il numero corrispondente a $\mathrm{IMO}$. L'aggiunta viene effettuata colonnalmente come mostrato (le cifre di ciascuna riga sono allineate a destra): $$\begin{array}{cccc} & I & M & O \\ & & & \& \\ & J & M & O \\ & & & \& \\ + & & & \& \\ \hline J & J & M & O \end{array}$$ Qui, risolvere un criptaritmo significa determinare la corrispondenza tra lettere e cifre, secondo le condizioni di seguito, in modo che l'uguaglianza di cui sopra sia valida. - ciascuna delle $I, M, O, \&$ corrisponde a una delle cifre $0, 1, 2, \ldots, 9$. La stessa lettera corrisponde alla stessa cifra, e lettere diverse corrisponde a cifre diverse. Pertanto $\mathrm{IMO}$ indica il numero $3$ di cifre formato scrivendo la cifra corrispondente a $I$, la cifra corrispondente a $M$ e la cifra corrispondente a $O$, in tale ordine. - Le prime lettere $I, \&, J$ non corrispondono a $0$. - La cifra corrispondente a $I$ non è necessariamente $1$, e la cifra corrispondente a $O$ non è necessariamente $0$.

*Fill-in dialogue: triples and the operation a*b mod 10*

> Fill in the blanks $\boxed{\text{(a)}}$ through $\boxed{\text{(h)}}$. Here, each of $\boxed{\text{(a)}}$ and $\boxed{\text{(b)}}$ is a triple of integers; $\boxed{\text{(c)}}$ and $\boxed{\text{(d)}}$ are single-digit positive integers; $\boxed{\text{(e)}}$, $\boxed{\text{(f)}}$, $\boxed{\text{(h)}}$ are nonnegative integers; and $\boxed{\text{(g)}}$ is several integers.
> 
> For integers $a, b$ that are at least $0$, let $a * b$ denote the remainder when $a \times b$ is divided by $10$. Consider the operation that sends a triple $(a, b, c)$ of integers from $0$ to $9$ to the triple $(a*b,\, b*c,\, c*a)$ of integers from $0$ to $9$. Here, as in $(2,7,0)$ and $(2,0,7)$, triples that differ only in the order of their entries are regarded as distinct triples. We want to investigate which triples can be produced by performing this operation exactly once.
> 
> Student A: "For example, from $(2,3,5)$ we can make $(6,5,0)$."
> Teacher: "Then, can we make $(3,4,2)$?"
> Student B: "It can be made from $\boxed{\text{(a)}}$ or $\boxed{\text{(b)}}$."
> Teacher: "What about $(4,3,7)$?"
> Student B: "Hmm... ah, I see! If $(4,3,7)$ can be made from $(a,b,c)$, then $a*b$ is a multiple of $\boxed{\text{(c)}}$, so $(a*b)\cdot(b*c)\cdot(c*a)$ would be a multiple of $\boxed{\text{(c)}}$."
> Student A: "But $3$ and $7$ are not multiples of $\boxed{\text{(c)}}$, so it cannot be done."
> Student B: "Thinking the same way, in general a triple in which exactly $\boxed{\text{(d)}}$ of the three entries is a multiple of $\boxed{\text{(c)}}$ cannot be made."
> Teacher: "Just now we focused on multiples of $\boxed{\text{(c)}}$, but the same kind of thing can be said by focusing on multiples of $\boxed{\text{(e)}}$."
> Student B: "So a triple in which exactly $\boxed{\text{(d)}}$ of the three entries is a multiple of $\boxed{\text{(e)}}$ cannot be made."
> Teacher: "Then what about $(4,3,4)$?"
> Student A: "Since the number of multiples of $\boxed{\text{(c)}}$ and the number of multiples of $\boxed{\text{(e)}}$ are both not $\boxed{\text{(d)}}$, it is bound to be makeable."
> Student B: "Wait a moment. If $(4,3,4)$ can be made from $(a,b,c)$, then the remainder of $a^2 b^2 c^2$ upon division by $5$ is $\boxed{\text{(f)}}$, isn't it? But there is no integer whose square has remainder $\boxed{\text{(f)}}$ upon division by $5$."
> Student A: "Since the only possible remainders when an integer is squared and divided by $5$ are $\boxed{\text{(g)}}$, we see that there are still other triples that cannot be made."
> Teacher: "Exactly. In fact, every triple other than those shown to be unmakeable by the discussion so far can be made. Student A, please verify this."
> Student A: (30 minutes later) "Done!"
> Student B: "In other words, the total number of triples that can be produced by this operation is $\boxed{\text{(h)}}$."
> Teacher: "That's right."

*Dialogo di riempimento: triples e l'operazione a*b mod 10*

> Riempire i vuoti $\boxed{\text{(a)}}$ fino a $\boxed{\text{(h)}}$. Qui, come in $(2,7,0)$ e $(2,0,7)$, i triples che differiscono solo nell'ordine delle loro voci sono considerati triples distinti. "Studente A: "Ma $3$ e $7$ non sono multipli di $\boxed{\text{(c)}}$, quindi non può essere fatto.
